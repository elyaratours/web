The pomegranate appears everywhere: on coats of arms, ironwork, fountains, facades, benches, shops and souvenirs. Sometimes it can look like one more tourist detail, but it is one of those small clues that helps you read the city.

The symbol connects name, identity and political memory. After the Christian conquest, the image of the pomegranate became part of the visual language of the new power. The city was not only occupied: it was also represented.

Noticing these details changes the walk. A facade stops being only stone, a fountain stops being only a place to rest, and a coat of arms becomes a short sentence about who wanted to tell Granada's story.

Next time you walk through the center, play at finding them. You will not have to look very hard. Granada, quietly, repeats itself.
